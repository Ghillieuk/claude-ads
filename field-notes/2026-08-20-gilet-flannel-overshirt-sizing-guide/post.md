## Body (paste-ready HTML)

<p>Every product page has a size chart. What it won't tell you is whether a gilet is supposed to sit close to the body or leave room for a jumper, or whether an overshirt is meant to fit like a shirt at all. Get that wrong and you end up either returning something that measured correctly on paper, or wearing it wrong for months without realising why it never quite feels right. This is less about chest measurements — check the size chart on each product page for those — and more about how each piece in the range is actually designed to sit.</p>

<h2>How the Torr Wool Gilet Should Fit</h2>
<p>A gilet has one job a jumper doesn't: it has to leave room for what you're wearing under it while still trapping the warmth in. If you're wearing the <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/collections/midlayers">Torr Wool Gilet</a> over a T-shirt or a single shirt layer, true to size is right — close enough across the shoulders and chest that it isn't flapping, with a straight drop rather than pulling at the zip when you sit down. If you know you'll mostly be wearing it over a flannel shirt or a midlayer, especially through the colder months, size up one from your usual chest measurement. A gilet that's too tight over bulk will ride up at the back and stop doing its job at the exact moment you need it most, while a gilet that's slightly loose over a thin base layer barely loses any warmth at all. The range runs S to 3XL — if you're between two sizes, go with the size that suits what you'll actually be layering underneath, not just your chest number in isolation.</p>

<h2>How a Flannel Shirt Should Fit</h2>
<p>Both the <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/collections/flannels">Brae and Munro Flannel Shirts</a> are cut generously through the body for freedom of movement, which is a deliberate choice rather than an accident of pattern-cutting — a flannel that's tight through the shoulders restricts exactly the reaching and bending a day outdoors tends to involve. That generous cut means most people find true to size gives a comfortably relaxed fit on its own, with enough room to move without looking baggy. The main decision point is whether you're planning to wear it as the outer layer or as one piece in a stack — worn on its own, true to size looks and moves the way it's meant to; worn under a gilet or overshirt, there's already enough room built in that you shouldn't need to size up again on top of it. Where fit actually varies is sleeve length rather than body — check the specific listing if you're tall or have longer arms, since that's the measurement most likely to feel short before the chest does.</p>

<h2>How the Hill Loch Overshirt Should Fit</h2>
<p>The <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/products/hill-loch-shirt-dark-denim">Hill Loch Shirt</a> is built to be worn open over a T-shirt or a lighter shirt, not buttoned up on its own the way a standard shirt is, and the fit reflects that. It runs with a slightly longer arm than a typical shirt, with an extra button on the arm gusset specifically so the sleeves roll and stay rolled rather than sliding back down — a small detail, but one that only makes sense once you know the shirt is meant to be worn open and rolled rather than closed and cuffed. True to size gives enough room in the body to sit comfortably over another layer without looking like it's a size too big; if you're planning to wear it buttoned as a standalone shirt more often than open as an overshirt, it's worth sizing down slightly, since the generous cut is built around the layering use case first.</p>

<h2>If You're Between Sizes</h2>
<p>The most reliable trick isn't guessing from a size chart at all — it's laying a gilet, flannel or shirt you already own flat and comparing its measurements to the chart on the product page you're looking at. That tells you more about real-world fit than a chest number on its own, because it accounts for how you actually prefer things to sit, close or relaxed. If you're still unsure, sizing up is the safer default for anything you plan to layer under or over, and sizing true to your measurement is the safer default for anything you plan to wear as a standalone piece. And if it still isn't right once it arrives, that's what the 14-day returns window is for — a size that looked correct on the chart doesn't always feel correct on, and swapping it is meant to be straightforward rather than a hassle.</p>

<p>Fit is really a question of what's going on underneath, not just what's on the label. Buying to layer under something else, size up; buying to wear on its own, trust the chart and go true to size.
