## Body (paste-ready HTML)

<p>A down jacket looks like it should go in the wash the same way as anything else — it's fabric, it gets muddy, it goes in the machine. Wash it like a flannel shirt or a wool gilet, though, and you'll end up with a jacket that's thinner in patches, clumped in others, and missing a fair bit of the loft that made it warm in the first place. Down asks for a different routine to almost everything else in a layering system, and it's worth getting right, because a badly washed down jacket doesn't recover.</p>

<h2>What's Actually in the Jacket</h2>
<p>Our <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/products/corrie-down-jacket">Corrie Down Jacket</a> is filled with 100% goose down inside a water-repellent outer shell, with a breathable inner lining that lets moisture move rather than sit against you. The down is doing the actual insulating — it works by trapping air in thousands of tiny clusters, and it only keeps you warm while those clusters stay separated and lofted. That's the whole reason the care routine here is worth reading properly rather than assuming it's the same as our <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/blogs/news/how-to-care-for-a-wool-gilet-a-no-fuss-guide">wool gilet</a> or a cotton shirt: wash down the wrong way and you collapse those clusters, sometimes for good.</p>

<h2>The Short Version</h2>
<p>Use a specialist down wash or a gentle non-biological detergent — never a standard powder or anything with fabric softener. Wash on a cold or 30°C gentle cycle, then run an extra rinse to make sure nothing's left in the fill. Dry on low heat in a tumble dryer with a few clean tennis balls or dryer balls to break up clumps, and be patient — it takes hours, not one cycle. Always check the swing tag on your own jacket first; this is general good practice for goose down, not a substitute for the manufacturer's specific instructions.</p>

<h2>Why Down Needs Different Handling</h2>
<p>Ordinary detergent and fabric softener leave a residue that coats the down plumes and stops them separating properly once dry — which is how a jacket comes out of a normal wash looking fine and performing like a much thinner one. Down also needs more rinsing than most fabrics to get fully clean, because soap trapped inside the fill is what causes the clumping people usually blame on the down itself. None of this is difficult, it's just different from how you'd treat a shirt, and skipping a step tends to show up as reduced warmth rather than anything visible straight away.</p>

<h2>Washing It Properly</h2>
<p>Empty the pockets, close the zips, and check for any small tears in the shell before it goes in — a loose seam under agitation is how you end up with feathers in the drum. Use a down-specific technical wash (Nikwax Down Wash and Grangers both make one) or, failing that, a small amount of gentle non-biological liquid detergent, and wash on a cold or 30°C cycle on your machine's gentlest setting, often labelled as a hand-wash or wool cycle. Run a second rinse cycle afterwards if your machine allows it — this is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters most for keeping the down from clumping later.</p>

<h2>Drying It Without Flattening the Fill</h2>
<p>This is where a down jacket takes longer than you'd expect. Tumble dry on low heat with two or three clean tennis balls or purpose-made dryer balls in with it — they knock the down clusters apart as they clump together wet, which is exactly what stops the jacket drying into flat patches. It can take several hours, and it's worth stopping the dryer every 20–30 minutes to pull the jacket out and give it a firm shake by hand, redistributing any fill that's bunched into a corner or a seam. Don't rush this by turning up the heat — high heat is what damages both the down and the shell fabric, and low-and-slow is genuinely the only way to get an even result.</p>

<h2>Bringing Back the Water Repellency</h2>
<p>Washing strips some of the water-repellent finish off the outer shell, even when you've done everything else right — that's true of any DWR-treated shell, not just down jackets. Once the jacket is completely dry, a wash-in or spray-on reproofing treatment (the same category of product as the down wash itself) restores the shell's ability to shed light rain rather than soak it up. It's a five-minute step and it's the difference between the jacket performing the way it did new and slowly wetting out over a few seasons.</p>

<h2>What Not to Do</h2>
<p>Skip standard dry cleaning — the solvents used can strip the natural oils out of the down and leave it brittle rather than lofted. Don't use bleach or fabric softener under any circumstances, and don't air dry a down jacket after washing; without tumbling and agitation the down dries into hard, flat clumps that won't loft back out on their own. If in doubt about a specific stain or a heavily soiled patch, spot clean it rather than putting the whole jacket through an extra wash cycle it doesn't need.</p>

<h2>Storing It Between Wears</h2>
<p>Hang it on a wide hanger or lay it loosely folded on a shelf — never leave a down jacket compressed in a stuff sack or under other coats for long periods, since sustained compression is what stops the fill lofting back up to full thickness over time. Between wears during the season, a quick shake and an airing somewhere ventilated is usually all it needs; save the full wash for when it's actually dirty or has started losing loft, not on a fixed schedule.</p>

<h2>The Field Notes Verdict</h2>
<p>A down jacket is genuinely low-maintenance once you know the routine — it's just not the same routine as the rest of your kit. Use a proper down wash, rinse it twice, dry it low and slow with something to break up the clumps, and reproof the shell once it's dry. Do that and the Corrie will still be lofted and warm in five winters, not two.
