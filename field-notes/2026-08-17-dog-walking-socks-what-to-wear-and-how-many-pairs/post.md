## Body (paste-ready HTML)

<p>Socks are the last thing anyone thinks about before a dog walk, and usually the first thing they regret once a field's turned to mud and a wet sock's working its way down inside a boot. It's not complicated kit, but it's easy to get wrong in ways that make an otherwise decent pair of boots miserable to walk in.</p>

<h2>Why Dog Walking Is Harder on Socks Than It Looks</h2>
<p>A dog walk isn't a two-hour hike with a fixed pace and a dry forecast — it's whatever the dog wants, in whatever weather turns up, often twice a day. That means standing about at the gate while the dog finishes sniffing something, crossing wet grass that a proper hiking route would avoid, and doing it again tomorrow whether yesterday's socks are properly dry or not. The sock doing that job needs to manage damp without holding onto it, and needs to survive being worn more often than a once-a-week hiking sock ever would.</p>

<h2>What to Actually Look For</h2>
<p>Skip cotton. It soaks up moisture and holds onto it, which is exactly what turns a cold morning into a cold, damp foot and, eventually, a blister. A wool-blend sock manages moisture properly and stays warmer even when it's picked up some damp, which is why it's the standard recommendation across country-clothing retailers for anything involving wet fields. Beyond the fibre, three things matter more than people expect: enough length in the leg to sit above the top of a boot rather than disappearing into it, some padding at the heel and toe so a long walk doesn't turn into a blister walk, and something at the arch to stop the sock migrating down inside the boot over the course of a walk — there's little more irritating than stopping halfway round a field to haul a sock back up.</p>

<h2>How Many Pairs You Actually Need</h2>
<p>Three is the practical minimum, and it's not an arbitrary number — it's one pair on, one pair drying from this morning's walk, and one pair genuinely dry and ready to go. Wool-blend socks need proper time to dry out fully between wears; wearing a pair that's still slightly damp from yesterday is how a decent sock starts to smell and lose its shape faster than it should. Two dog walks a day, which is normal for plenty of dogs, makes a strong case for going a pair or two beyond that if you can, simply so nothing's ever going back on still damp.</p>

<h2>Keeping Them in Rotation</h2>
<p>Wash them cold rather than hot — a hot wash is harder on the elastic that keeps a sock's arch fit and its shape, and that elastic is doing real work here. Let them dry properly rather than reaching for the tumble dryer's high heat setting, for the same reason. None of this is precious care — it's the same basic logic as looking after any wool-blend knit, just applied to something that's on your feet most days rather than folded in a drawer.</p>
