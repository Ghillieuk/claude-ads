<p>Most overshirt buying advice online is really just style advice — how to wear one, what to wear it with. Useful, but it skips the part that actually determines whether the thing earns its place in your wardrobe: whether it's cut, woven and fastened to do the job in the first place. Fit, fabric weight and fastening are the three things worth checking before you buy. Here's what to look for in each, and how our own range stacks up.</p>

<h2>Fit: Room to Layer, Not Room to Swim</h2>
<p>An overshirt has one job a normal shirt doesn't — sitting over another layer without pulling at the shoulders or binding at the arms. That means a bit more ease through the chest and back than you'd want in a shirt worn on its own, and sleeves cut with enough room to go over a T-shirt or fine knit. Too roomy and it stops looking like a shirt at all; too close-fitting and it can only ever be a shirt, whatever the label calls it. Length matters too — long enough in the body and tail that it doesn't ride up when you reach or bend, especially if you're wearing it open.</p>
<p>Our <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/products/hill-loch-shirt">Hill Loch Shirt</a> is built with exactly this in mind: full through the chest and shoulders, tapered rather than boxy through the body, with a tail long enough to stay put and sleeves with enough ease to layer without binding. The <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/products/munro-brushed-cotton-shirt">Munro Brushed Cotton Shirt</a> runs slightly more fitted through the chest and long through the body — worth knowing if you want a closer layer under a gilet rather than a roomier one over a tee.</p>

<h2>Weight and Fabric: Matching the Season</h2>
<p>Fabric weight decides what an overshirt is actually for. Something heavy and close-woven — a proper twill or a double-brushed flannel — holds its shape as an outer layer and keeps the wind off; something lighter is better suited to being worn buttoned as a shirt in its own right, or as a warm-weather layer that doesn't add much bulk. Neither is wrong, but buying the wrong one for the season you need it in is the most common overshirt mistake.</p>
<p>Across our range, the three shirts sit at genuinely different weights. The Hill Loch is a medium-weight cotton twill — dense enough to resist wind and light rain, light enough not to overheat you on the walk there. The <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/products/brae-flannel-shirt">Brae Flannel Shirt</a> is a heavier, double-brushed 100% cotton flannel built for genuine cold-weather warmth. The Munro sits between the two — a brushed cotton with more give than the flannel but more substance than the twill. None of them are trying to be the other; picking between them is really picking the weight you need.</p>

<h2>Fastening: Buttons, Poppers and Pockets</h2>
<p>The small hardware details tell you how much thought went into a garment being worn as an outer layer rather than tucked in under a jacket all day. A hidden button under the collar stops it flapping in the wind. Chest pockets that close properly — a popper, a zip, a genuine button flap — matter more on an overshirt than a normal shirt, because it's the layer things actually get carried in: phone, keys, dog treats.</p>
<p>The Hill Loch's chest pocket closes with a zip and popper together, which is about as secure as a shirt pocket gets. The Brae Flannel's two chest pockets each have a sunglasses-and-accessories holder compartment built in, and a hidden button under the collar to stop fly-aways. The Munro's utility pockets use inverted pleats for extra room and come with a microfibre sunglass-cleaning cloth built into the hem lining — a genuinely useful detail if you're out and back in changeable light all day.</p>
