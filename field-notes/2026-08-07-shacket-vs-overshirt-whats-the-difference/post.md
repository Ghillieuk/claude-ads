## Body (paste-ready HTML)

<p>Shacket or overshirt — scroll through enough menswear sites and you'll see both words slapped on what looks like the exact same garment, sometimes on the same page. Neither term is wrong, and neither is especially precise. Here's what's actually going on, and whether it's worth caring about before you buy one.</p>

<h2>So What's the Actual Difference?</h2>
<p>"Shacket" is a portmanteau — shirt plus jacket — for a garment that keeps a shirt's shape (collar, button front, chest pockets) but is cut from something heavier: a proper flannel, a brushed twill, sometimes a light wool blend. "Overshirt" describes much the same idea from the other direction — a shirt cut roomier and thicker than your usual button-up, specifically so it can go over another layer rather than under one. In practice, the two words get used interchangeably by most retailers, us included until we sat down to write this. If you're being pedantic about it: a shacket sits closer to the jacket end of the scale, an overshirt closer to the shirt end. Both do the same job.</p>

<h2>Where the Line Actually Sits</h2>
<p>The useful distinction isn't the name, it's the weight and the cut. A true overshirt is built to be worn open over a T-shirt or fine knit, sized generously enough through the shoulders and body to layer without pulling. A shacket tends to run a touch shorter and boxier, closer to a jacket's proportions, and some come with a genuine lining. Fabric is the giveaway either way — if it's heavier than something you'd wear buttoned to the collar on its own, it's designed to be a layer, not a shirt with ideas above its station.</p>
<p>None of this changes how you'd actually wear one. It's a fit-and-fabric question, not a rulebook, and any UK menswear site running a "shacket vs overshirt" piece will tell you the same thing in different words.</p>
