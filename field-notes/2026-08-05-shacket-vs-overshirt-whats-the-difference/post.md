## Body (paste-ready HTML)

<p>Shacket. Overshirt. Shirt jacket. Three names doing the rounds for what might be the same garment, or three genuinely different things, depending who you ask and which shop you're stood in. Here's what actually separates them, and when the distinction is worth paying attention to.</p>

<h2>Is There Actually a Difference?</h2>
<p>Mostly not. Both describe the same basic idea: a shirt cut from something heavier than shirting fabric, such as twill, brushed cotton or a wool blend, worn open over a t-shirt or buttoned as a light outer layer. Retailers swap the two words freely, often for the exact same garment on different pages of the same site. If a shop tells you their "shacket" is a fundamentally different product to their "overshirt," they're usually selling you on a label, not a genuine construction difference.</p>

<h2>Where the Two Terms Diverge in Practice</h2>
<p>Where it's worth paying attention is fabric weight and intended use. "Shacket" tends to get used for the heavier, boxier end: sometimes lined, cut roomy enough to go over a jumper, and built to be the outermost layer on a mild day. "Overshirt" usually sits lighter, closer to a proper shirt in cut, unlined, designed to layer under a coat as often as it's worn alone.</p>
<p>Check the weight and the lining before the label. A heavier, unlined or lightly lined piece that's meant to stand on its own in cool weather is functioning as a shacket, whatever the tag says. A lighter, trimmer piece that's happiest tucked under something else is functioning as an overshirt, and buying one expecting the other is where most of the disappointment in this category comes from.</p>

<h2>Why the Distinction Is Worth Knowing</h2>
<p>It matters because the two do different jobs in a British autumn. An overshirt earns its place as a bridge layer: over a t-shirt on a mild morning, under a coat once the wind picks up, out of the way entirely by midday. Ask it to work as your only layer on a properly cold walk and it comes up short. A heavier shacket does more of that work alone, at the cost of being too warm to layer comfortably once the temperature climbs.</p>
<p>Neither is the "correct" choice. It depends on how many layers you actually want to be managing on a given day, and whether the piece needs to double as a midlayer under an <a href="https://ghillieuk.com/collections/outerwear">outer jacket</a> or stand in for one.</p>

<h2>The Field Notes Verdict</h2>
<p>Don't get hung up on which word is printed on the label. Shacket and overshirt describe the same category, and either term will get you understood.
